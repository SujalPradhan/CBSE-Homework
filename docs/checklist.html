1. Executive summary

Problem. A chatbot can now write a complete, convincing answer to most worksheet homework. India's curriculum framework names this risk directly: students "outsourcing" thinking to AI through "homework and assignments being done by AI" (NCF-SE 2023, PDF p.193).

Proposed solution. Five homework tasks built on activities already in the NCERT books. In each one the student predicts, then measures or tests something at home, then explains, then teaches it back to a family member. The website shows the tasks directly. Students need no login, and there is nothing to grade online. A teacher sends one link and spends 2 minutes the next day on a fresh-case question in class.

What it does and does not do. None of the tasks is AI-proof. In our red-team, a chatbot wrote a plausible answer sheet for all five. The value is that an honest student learns from doing it, and that the in-class check shows who did. That second point is untested until a real classroom trial.

2. Research findings

FindingImplication for design
NCF-SE 2023 says homework "should not be intended to merely repeat what has been learnt in class, but rather to apply it to different contexts", must be age-appropriate, and students should be able to do it "on their own" without parents doing it for them. (NCF-SE 2023 §3.3.7, printed p.91)Tasks apply a textbook idea in the student's own home. A family member is the audience for teach-back, not a helper.
The same framework warns that AI doing homework "starts stunting the growth of the capacities outsourced" and says "nothing more definitive can be said" yet. (NCF-SE 2023, PDF p.193)No official CBSE rule on AI and homework exists to design against, so we use Anand's tests (section 3).
In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school students in Turkey, unrestricted GPT-4 raised practice scores by 48% but lowered later unassisted exam scores by 17%. A tutor version that avoided giving answers largely removed the harm. (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025)AI that hands out answers can hurt learning. Our feedback hints never give the answer. The real check happens without AI.
Students who predicted the outcome of a demonstration before seeing it understood it significantly better; students who only watched did no better than those who never saw it. (Crouch et al., Am. J. Phys. 2004)Every task starts with a prediction, which the student locks before the activity.
Learning rises as engagement moves from passive to active to constructive to interactive. (Chi & Wylie 2014, the ICAP framework; the authors present it as a hypothesis, and at least one later lab study found the opposite order)Explanation questions and teach-back are "constructive" and "interactive"; copying an answer is "passive".
Homework shows a positive link with achievement, and the correlation is stronger in Grades 7–12 than K–6, though all studies had design flaws. US evidence. (Cooper, Robinson & Patall 2006)Homework is worth doing at this age, as long as it is meaningful.
In rural India, 82.2% of 14–16-year-olds can use a smartphone; of those, 57% used it for education in the past week. Almost 90% have one at home. (ASER 2024 via PIB; rural only, and ages 14–16 not 11–14)Assume a shared parent phone at best. Every task has a printable paper version, and the activity itself needs no device.

Curriculum check: every task was checked against the current NCERT PDFs (all marked Reprint 2026-27) from ncert.nic.in; page references here. Gap: the CBSE circulars on adopting the new NCERT books (Acad-29/2024, Acad-12/2025) returned "404 Not Found" from cbseacademic.nic.in, so CBSE's adoption of these books is not confirmed here from CBSE's own documents.

3. Principles → requirements

From Anand S, The Exam Is a Hypothesis and the TDS ideation session (12 Oct 2026). Those tests were written for graded online assessment; for homework we keep the intent and drop the grading machinery.

PrincipleRequirement for these assignments
Learning testWhat the student must do personally (measure, predict, taste, explain) is the skill itself, not busywork.
Forward testAnswers depend on the student's own home, so copying a friend's sheet does not make sense, and the class check exposes it.
Solver testNo single shared answer exists: everyone's measurements differ.
Agent testAccept that AI can write the sheet. Put the check in class (the "presence" pattern), not in the website.
Faking testFaking should take as much thinking as the lesson (e.g. faking the paper task means dividing decimals).
Oracle testNot used for grading. Feedback hints only check whether numbers agree with each other, and never give the answer.
Explain-back (7th test)Every task ends with teaching a family member and writing what they say back.
Patterns that last: identity, real-world data, interaction, presenceOwn home and family (identity); predict-then-measure (real-world data); family taster and class comparison (interaction); the 2-minute class check (presence).

What we changed after it

  • Science error fixed: AI sentence 4 originally said hot and cold water dissolve the same amount of salt. That is nearly true for common salt, so a student testing it would "confirm" the AI. It now says temperature never matters for any solid, and the teacher notes suggest testing with sugar, with an adult handling hot water.
  • Feedback bugs fixed: a wrong "measure in mm, not cm" hint; a pages-vs-sheets check that missed the mistake; a pendulum hint that gave away the answer; salt feedback that declared "saturated" for any number.
  • Personal detail added: what was measured and what was on it; the book's name and last page; the taster's own words; what the leftover salt did when more water was added.
  • Real data asked for: two raw timings per pendulum length, not just an average.
  • Pendulum made a direct test of the prediction: 25 cm → 100 cm (4×), matching the "4 times longer" prediction.
  • A student-designed case: drink D, so there is no single answer to copy.
  • "Working with a friend" rule on each task: each student does it at home, then you compare results.
  • Access and safety: a 15 cm ruler is fine; use a book with 200+ pages; an adult ties the thread; colour instead of taste if sugar is not allowed.

What remains

  • An AI can still invent believable numbers and a family member's sentence. The website cannot tell; the 2-minute class check and reading sentences aloud are the defence.
  • The red-team judged the revisions from the text; they have not been re-tested by a second AI pass or by real students.
  • Where faking still teaches: partly in paper (dividing decimals) and sweetness (scaling); barely in pendulum and salt, where faking needs facts rather than reasoning.

If AI feedback is added later: give feedback only on the "Explain" answers, with a tutor-style prompt that asks questions and does not give answers (the setup that avoided harm in Bastani et al.). Limit cost per answer sheet rather than per student: one feedback request per question, plus one more after a revision, cached by the exact text. Use a small model and a short prompt. Students can still revise as much as they like without new requests.

An earlier prototype had class codes, PINs and server grading. It was removed because the red-team showed server checks cannot stop AI-made numbers, and it added setup for teachers.

9. Validation plan

Pilot: 2–3 teachers, one class each, one task per week for 3 weeks. Before starting, each teacher picks the task. No results are claimed until this is done.

QuestionMeasureCompletion or learning?
Did they learn it?Share of students answering the 2-minute fresh-case check correctly, compared with a similar class given the usual worksheetLearning
Can they explain it?Rubric level (Got it / Partly / Not yet) on 10 random notebooks per classLearning
Does AI use help or hurt?Anonymous one-question survey ("Did you use AI? For what?") crossed with the class-check resultLearning
Does collaboration help?Teacher notes on whether pairs compared different results or copiedLearning

10. Next steps

  1. First: Anand or a teacher reviews the five tasks for wording and grade fit. Then run the pilot above with one task (handspans or pendulum are the easiest to start with).
  2. Then: fix what the pilot shows; add a Grade 6 Maths task; translate into Hindi.
  3. Can wait: AI feedback on explanations; pooled class results.
  4. Exclude from the first prototype: logins, dashboards, online grading, anything that tracks students.
